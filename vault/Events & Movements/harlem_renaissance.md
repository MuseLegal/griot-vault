## Summary

The Harlem Renaissance (c. 1918–mid-1930s) was a cultural, social, and artistic explosion centered in Harlem, New York City, spanning the 1920s and early 1930s. It is often called the "New Negro Movement" and represented the first major African American literary and artistic movement. The Renaissance celebrated Black culture, music, literature, and visual arts, challenging racist stereotypes and establishing African American cultural expression as a significant force in American art and letters.

## Historical Context

The Harlem Renaissance emerged from several converging factors:

- **The Great Migration (1910s–1930s):** Millions of African Americans moved from the rural South to Northern cities, including Harlem, seeking economic opportunities and escaping Jim Crow segregation.
- **World War I:** Black soldiers who fought in Europe returned with a renewed sense of pride and expectation for equality.
- **The "New Negro" ideology:** Alain Locke's 1925 anthology *The New Negro: An Interpretation* articulated a new sense of self-respect and self-dependence among African Americans.

## Key Figures

### Literature
- **Langston Hughes (1902–1967):** Poet, novelist, and playwright whose works like "The Weary Blues" (1926) and "Montage of a Dream Deferred" captured the rhythms of Black life. His poems incorporated jazz and blues influences.
- **Zora Neale Hurston (1891–1960):** Novelist and anthropologist whose *Their Eyes Were Watching God* (1937) is considered a masterpiece of African American literature. She collected African American folklore from the South.
- **Countee Cullen (1903–1946):** Poet whose work "Color" (1925) explored racial identity.
- **Claude McKay (1889–1948):** Jamaican-born poet whose "Harlem Shadows" (1922) and "If We Must Die" (1919) became anthems of Black resistance.
- **Weldon Heyburn (1899–1953):** Novelist and playwright whose *Cane* (1923) is considered the first major work of the Harlem Renaissance.

### Music
- **Duke Ellington (1899–1974):** Composer and bandleader whose performances at the Cotton Club (1927–1932) brought jazz to white audiences. His compositions like "Black and Tan Fantasy" and "Mood Indigo" expanded jazz's artistic possibilities.
- **Louis Armstrong (1901–1971):** Trumpeter and vocalist whose innovations in jazz improvisation defined the genre.
- **Bessie Smith (1894–1937):** "Empress of the Blues," whose recordings like "St. Louis Blues" (1925) influenced generations of musicians.
- **Fletcher Henderson (1897–1952):** Bandleader and arranger who helped define the swing era.

### Visual Arts
- **Aaron Douglas (1899–1979):** Painter and illustrator whose murals and book illustrations defined the visual aesthetic of the Harlem Renaissance.
- **Palmer Hayden (1890–1973):** Painter known for "The Janitor Who Painted" and other works depicting Black life.
- **Augusta Savage (1892–1952):** Sculptor whose *Gamin* (1929) and *The Negro Speaks of Rivers* (1930s) were celebrated works.
- **James Van Der Zandt (1886–1956): Photographer who documented Harlem's cultural elite.

## The Cotton Club and Jazz

The Cotton Club, a Harlem nightclub open to white patrons only, became the most famous venue for Harlem Renaissance jazz. Duke Ellington's residency (1927–1932) and performances by Louis Armstrong, Fats Waller, and others brought jazz to national prominence. Jazz became the defining musical form of the era, influencing classical music, popular culture, and global artistic movements.

## Legacy and Decline

The Harlem Renaissance declined with the Great Depression (1929), which reduced patronage for Black artists. However, its legacy is enormous:

- **Literary:** Established African American literature as a major force in American letters.
- **Musical:** Jazz became America's most important musical contribution to world culture.
- **Cultural:** The "New Negro" ideology influenced subsequent civil rights movements.
- **International:** The movement influenced African and Caribbean intellectuals, including Léopold Sédar Senghor's Negritude movement.
- **Institutional:** The Renaissance led to the establishment of Black studies programs and African American cultural institutions.

## Significance

The Harlem Renaissance represents the first major African American cultural movement to achieve national and international recognition. It demonstrated the artistic genius of Black Americans, challenged racist stereotypes, and established cultural expression as a form of political resistance. The movement's emphasis on racial pride, cultural heritage, and artistic innovation continues to influence African American and global culture today.
