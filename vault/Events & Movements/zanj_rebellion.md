## Summary

The Zanj Rebellion (869–883 CE) was a massive uprising of enslaved Zanj — East African slaves trafficked through the Indian Ocean slave trade — in the marshlands of southern Iraq, within the Abbasid Caliphate. Led by al-Hassan ibn Salih, the rebellion grew into a sustained military campaign that captured the city of Basra and threatened the Abbasid capital, and it lasted fourteen years, one of the longest and most consequential slave revolts in recorded history.

## Historical Context

The Abbasid Caliphate began importing enslaved Zanj labor from East Africa in the 8th century to drain the marshlands of southern Iraq for rice cultivation. Working conditions were brutal and mortality among the enslaved was high. By the late 9th century, an estimated 40,000–50,000 enslaved Zanj had been brought into the region.

## The Rebellion

Al-Hassan ibn Salih, who claimed descent from a Persian noble family and was himself of East African origin, emerged as the rebellion's leader in the 870s. The Zanj established a fortified capital at al-Kadimiya, a city built on the marshes, and waged a prolonged guerrilla war against Abbasid forces. At its height, the rebellion controlled significant portions of the marshland region and posed a serious threat to Basra, one of the Caliphate's most important cities.

The Abbasids eventually suppressed the rebellion through a combination of military force and the use of enslaved Zanj against each other. Al-Hassan was killed in 883 CE, and the surviving Zanj were dispersed.

## Legacy

The Zanj Rebellion is remembered as one of the largest and most sustained slave uprisings in world history, a direct challenge to the slave-labor system that underpinned the Abbasid economy in southern Iraq. It is also a key reference point in the study of the Indian Ocean slave trade and of African agency in the medieval period.
