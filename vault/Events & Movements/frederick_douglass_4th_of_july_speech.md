Frederick Douglass's 1852 oration "What to the Slave is the Fourth of July," delivered to the New England Anti-Slavery Society in Rochester, New York, is one of the most incisive American political speeches of the 19th century. Speaking at the July 5 anniversary of the Declaration of Independence, Douglass turned the national holiday into a searing indictment of the distance between the republic's founding ideals and the lived reality of enslaved Black people. He concluded that, for the enslaved, the Fourth of July was not a celebration but "a declaration to every human heart in the land that they have no part or portion in its celebration" — a line that has made the speech a permanent touchstone of American civil-rights rhetoric.
