## Summary

The Black Codes were a series of laws passed by Southern states between 1865 and 1867, in the immediate aftermath of the Civil War and the abolition of slavery. The codes were designed to restrict the freedom, labor, and civil rights of newly freed African Americans, effectively recreating conditions of forced labor and social subordination. They became a central target of the Reconstruction-era Congress, which passed the Civil Rights Act of 1866 and the Fourteenth and Fifteenth Amendments in direct response.

## Key Provisions

Black Code laws typically included:

- **Labor contracts**: Freed people were required to sign annual labor contracts; refusing to sign or leaving a job before contract expiration was treated as a criminal offense, often punished by forced labor on private land.
- **Vagrancy laws**: Being without visible means of support was criminalized, providing a mechanism to arrest and "hire out" unemployed Black people to private employers.
- **Apprenticeship statutes**: Orphaned or formerly enslaved children could be "apprenticed" to their former masters or to white employers, effectively extending child labor bondage.
- **Racial restrictions**: Black people were barred from certain occupations, from owning firearms, from traveling without passes, and from testifying against white people in court.

## Impact and Aftermath

The Black Codes generated widespread outrage in the North and in the Republican-controlled Congress, contributing to the passage of the Reconstruction Acts of 1867 and the Fourteenth Amendment (1868), which guaranteed equal protection under the law, and the Fifteenth Amendment (1870), which protected voting rights. The codes also laid the legal and social groundwork for the later Jim Crow system of the late 19th and early 20th centuries.
