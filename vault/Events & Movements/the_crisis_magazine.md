## Summary

*The Crisis: A Record of the Darker Races* is the official magazine of the National Association for the Advancement of Colored People (NAACP) and the nation's oldest African American publication. Founded in November 1910 by W.E.B. Du Bois, who served as editor until 1934, *The Crisis* became the most influential African American publication of the early 20th century. Under Du Bois's editorship, it published works by Langston Hughes, Countee Cullen, Zora Neale Hurston, and other Harlem Renaissance writers, while exposing lynchings and advocating for civil rights legislation.

## Founding and Early Years

The NAACP was founded in 1909 by an interracial group of activists including W.E.B. Du Bois, Mary White Ovington, Oswald Garrison Villard, and Mary Church Terrell. The name "The Crisis" was inspired by James Russell Lowell's poem "The Present Crisis," written during the Civil War.

The first issue appeared in November 1910, with Du Bois writing: "The goal of this publication is to set forth those facts and arguments which show the danger of race prejudice, particularly as manifested today toward colored people."

## Du Bois's Editorship (1910–1934)

Under Du Bois's leadership, *The Crisis* grew from a small pamphlet to a national publication:

- **Circulation:** Grew from 1,000 copies in 1910 to over 100,000 copies per month by 1920.
- **Content:** Articles on current events, editorial commentary, short stories, poems, art, and reports on Black achievements worldwide.
- **Harlem Renaissance:** *The Crisis* was the primary platform for Harlem Renaissance writers, publishing works by Langston Hughes, Countee Cullen, Claude McKay, and Zora Neale Hurston.
- **Anti-lynching:** Du Bois used *The Crisis* to expose lynchings, publishing photographs and statistics that challenged the myth that lynching was a response to Black criminality.

## Key Contributions

### Literary Platform
*The Crisis* published the first works of many now-canonical African American writers:
- Langston Hughes's early poetry
- Countee Cullen's "Heritage" (1925)
- Claude McKay's "If We Must Die" (1919)
- Zora Neale Hurston's short fiction

### Political Advocacy
- Documented lynchings and racial violence.
- Advocated for anti-lynching legislation.
- Supported voting rights and desegregation.
- Reported on Pan-African movements and African independence.

### Cultural Documentation
*The Crisis* served as a comprehensive record of African American life, culture, and achievement during a period when such documentation was rare or nonexistent in mainstream media.

## Legacy

*The Crisis* remains in publication today (over 110 years), making it the longest-running African American magazine in U.S. history. Its legacy includes:

- **Journalistic excellence:** Setting standards for African American journalism.
- **Literary discovery:** Launching the careers of Harlem Renaissance writers.
- **Civil rights advocacy:** Documenting and challenging racial injustice.
- **Cultural preservation:** Creating an archive of African American life and culture.
- **Continuity:** Still published monthly, adapting to contemporary issues while maintaining its mission.

## Significance

*The Crisis* represents the most important African American publication in U.S. history. It served as the voice of the NAACP, the platform for the Harlem Renaissance, and the record of the civil rights movement. Its 110+ years of continuous publication make it an irreplaceable archive of African American history, culture, and political struggle.
