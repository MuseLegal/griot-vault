## Summary

The Mali Empire (c. 1235–1670) was one of the largest, wealthiest, and most culturally influential empires in African history. Founded by Sundiata Keita after his victory at the Battle of Kirina (1235), it controlled vast territories across West Africa, including parts of modern-day Mali, Guinea, Senegal, Gambia, Burkina Faso, and Niger. The empire is renowned for its wealth (particularly gold and salt), its patronage of Islamic scholarship, and its legendary ruler Mansa Musa.

## Founding: Sundiata Keita (c. 1217–1255)

Sundiata Keita (also known as Mari Djata) founded the Mali Empire after defeating the Sosso king Soumaoro Kanté at the Battle of Kirina in 1235. His story is preserved in the *Epic of Sundiata*, one of Africa's most important oral traditions, which recounts his miraculous recovery from childhood disability, his exile, and his eventual return to liberate the Mandinka people from Sosso domination.

Under Sundiata, Mali established the *Gbara*, a grand assembly of representatives from all parts of the empire, which served as a form of early democratic governance. The empire's constitution, the *Kouroukan Fouga*, established laws governing social order, resource distribution, and human rights — including regulations on marriage, adoption, and environmental conservation.

## Mansa Musa and the Golden Age (r. 1312–1337)

Mansa Musa (c. 1280–1337), who ruled from 1312 to 1337, is widely considered the greatest ruler of the Mali Empire and one of the wealthiest individuals in history. His famous pilgrimage to Mecca in 1324 demonstrated Mali's extraordinary wealth: he distributed so much gold in Cairo that he temporarily depressed the value of the metal in Egypt.

During his reign, Mali reached its territorial and cultural zenith:
- He commissioned the construction of the Djinguereber Mosque in Timbuktu, designed by the Andalusian architect al-Sahili.
- He established Timbuktu as a major center of Islamic learning and trade.
- The empire controlled roughly half of the Old World's known gold supply.
- Mansa Musa's pilgrimage put Mali on European and Middle Eastern maps, featured prominently in the 1375 Catalan Atlas.

## Economy and Trade

Mali's wealth derived from its control of trans-Saharan trade routes:
- **Gold:** Mali controlled the Wangara gold fields, supplying much of the Mediterranean and Middle Eastern gold markets.
- **Salt:** Extracted from Taghaza and Taoudenni, salt was traded for gold at favorable rates (sometimes a salt bar was worth its weight in gold).
- **Other commodities:** Ivory, kola nuts, cloth, and slaves moved along these routes.

The empire's economic prosperity enabled the flourishing of urban centers, particularly Timbuktu, Djenne, and Gao.

## Decline

The Mali Empire began its gradual decline after the death of Mansa Musa. Successive weak rulers, internal succession disputes, and the rise of competing powers (notably the Songhai Empire under Sunni Ali Ber) eroded Mali's territorial holdings. By the 16th century, much of the former empire had been absorbed by Songhai. The empire's remnants persisted in fragmented form until the late 17th century.

## Cultural Legacy

Mali's cultural achievements include:
- The development of the Mandinka language and oral tradition.
- The *Epic of Sundiata*, recognized by UNESCO as part of humanity's intangible cultural heritage.
- The spread of Islam throughout West Africa, with Mali's rulers serving as models of Islamic kingship.
- Architectural innovations, including the distinctive Sudano-Sahelian style of mosques in Timbuktu and Djenne.

## Significance

The Mali Empire represents one of Africa's greatest civilizational achievements. Its control of trans-Saharan trade made it one of the wealthiest states of the medieval world, while its patronage of Islamic scholarship and architecture left a lasting cultural legacy. Mansa Musa's pilgrimage demonstrated Africa's wealth to the wider world, challenging European narratives of African poverty and backwardness.
