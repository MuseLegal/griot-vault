## Summary
The Kingdom of Benin (fl. 13th–19th centuries) was a major West African kingdom centered in present-day Edo State, Nigeria. Its capital, Benin City, was renowned for its sophisticated urban planning, fortifications, and the royal palace decorated with thousands of brass and bronze sculptures. The artworks — known collectively as the Benin Bronzes (though mostly brass) — were created by hereditary guilds of brass casters under the Oba (king).

## Context
By the 15th century, the Oba was the supreme political, judicial, and spiritual leader. The kingdom traded ivory, pepper, and later slaves with Portuguese and Dutch traders from the 1490s onward. The "Golden Ages" of Benin metalwork occurred during the reigns of Oba Esigie (fl. 1550) and Oba Eresoyen (1735–1750). The lost-wax casting technique was introduced from Ife tradition in the 13th century; Benin artisans refined it to cast plaques only 3mm thick, surpassing contemporary European techniques.

## Significance
The Benin Bronzes are among the finest examples of African sculpture using lost-wax casting. They document court life, dynastic history, and relationships with neighboring kingdoms. Contrary to early 20th-century scholars, metallurgy in Benin predates Portuguese contact by centuries. The artworks were looted by the British army during the 1897 Punitive Expedition and are now dispersed across museums worldwide, fueling ongoing restitution debates.
