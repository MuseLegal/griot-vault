## Summary

The Great Zimbabwe soapstone "Zimbabwe Birds" are eight carved sculptures discovered at the ruins of Great Zimbabwe, a medieval city in modern-day Zimbabwe. Dating from the 12th to 15th centuries, these steatite (soapstone) carvings depict mythological birds, possibly combining features of different species. They are the national emblem of Zimbabwe, appearing on the country's flag, coat of arms, and currency.

## Great Zimbabwe: The City

Great Zimbabwe was the capital of the Kingdom of Great Zimbabwe (c. 1100–1450), a powerful Shona-state that controlled trade between the African interior and the Swahili coast. The city covers approximately 800 hectares and includes three main architectural complexes:

### The Great Enclosure
The largest structure, with walls up to 11 meters (36 feet) high — the largest ancient structure in sub-Saharan Africa. Built entirely of dry stone (no mortar), it contains the famous Cone Tower and the Valley Ruins.

### The Hill Complex
The oldest part, believed to have been the residence of the king and spiritual leaders.

### The Valley Ruins
A collection of smaller structures that may have housed the royal court and administrators.

## The Zimbabwe Birds

Eight soapstone bird sculptures have been discovered at Great Zimbabwe, each standing approximately 40–60 cm tall. They are carved from dark green soapstone (steatite) and rest on stone pedestals. Each bird features:

- A body resembling a baboon or eagle
- Wings folded against the body
- A distinctive "breast" pattern
- A small head with a possible crest

Scholars debate their exact meaning:
- **Arthur Hallam Mortlock (1902):** First proposed they represented the batele eagle.
- **David Randall-MacIver (1905):** Argued they were abstract representations, not literal birds.
- **Gerard von Grönne (1920s):** Suggested they depicted the African fish eagle.
- **Modern scholarship:** Generally agrees they represent mythological or symbolic birds, possibly representing ancestral spirits or messengers between the living and the dead.

## Historical Significance

Great Zimbabwe was built by the ancestors of the Shona people, who are still the dominant ethnic group in modern Zimbabwe. The city's construction spanned roughly 350 years (c. 1100–1450) and required enormous labor and organizational skill. At its peak, the city may have housed 18,000 people.

European denial of African achievement in the 19th and early 20th centuries led to racist theories that the city must have been built by Phoenicians, Arabs, or Egyptians — theories thoroughly debunked by archaeological evidence.

## Modern Legacy

The Zimbabwe Birds are the national symbol of Zimbabwe:
- They appear on the Zimbabwean flag (adopted 1980)
- They are featured on the coat of arms
- They appear on Zimbabwean currency
- They are used as the emblem of Zimbabwe's national soccer team

The name "Zimbabwe" itself derives from the Shona words *dzimba dza mabwe*, meaning "houses of stone."

## Significance

The Great Zimbabwe soapstone Birds represent one of Africa's most iconic artistic achievements. They demonstrate the sophisticated artistic and engineering capabilities of pre-colonial African civilizations, challenging colonial narratives that denied African agency in building great civilizations. As Zimbabwe's national emblem, they connect the modern nation to its deep historical roots.
