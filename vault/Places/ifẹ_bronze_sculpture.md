## Summary
The Ife Bronze Sculpture (fl. 12th–15th centuries) represents one of the most sophisticated traditions of naturalistic figurative art in African history. Created by the Yoruba people in present-day Nigeria, these sculptures were made using the lost-wax casting technique and are renowned for their naturalistic human forms, intricate detail, and technical mastery.

## Context
The city of Ife (modern-day Osun State, Nigeria) was the spiritual and cultural center of the Yoruba people. By the 12th century, Ife artisans had developed advanced metalworking skills, creating brass and bronze sculptures of remarkable naturalism. The sculptures depict royal figures, deities, and courtiers with detailed facial features, scarification marks, and elaborate regalia. The lost-wax casting technique was introduced from Ife tradition in the 13th century and later adopted by the Kingdom of Benin.

## Significance
The Ife sculptures demonstrate that naturalistic figurative art existed in Africa centuries before European contact. They challenge colonial-era assumptions about African art and demonstrate sophisticated technical knowledge. The sculptures are considered masterpieces of world art and have influenced modern African artists worldwide. They remain central to Yoruba cultural identity and spiritual practice.
