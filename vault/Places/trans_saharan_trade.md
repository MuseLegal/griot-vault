## Summary

Trans-Saharan trade refers to the network of trade routes crossing the Sahara Desert that connected West Africa to the Mediterranean world and the Middle East from approximately the 8th to the 16th centuries. These routes facilitated the exchange of gold, salt, ivory, kola nuts, and enslaved people, transforming West African economies and enabling the rise of powerful empires including Ghana, Mali, and Songhai.

## Historical Development

The trans-Saharan trade system emerged in the 8th century with the introduction of the camel to North Africa by Arab traders. Camels could carry heavy loads (up to 300 kg) and travel long distances without water, making them ideal for crossing the Sahara. By the 9th century, well-established trade routes connected:

- **Northern terminus:** Sijilmasa (Morocco) and other North African cities
- **Southern terminus:** Ghana, Mali, and Songhai kingdoms
- **Key intermediaries:** Timbuktu, Gao, Djenne, and Awka

## Major Commodities

### Gold
West Africa controlled approximately half of the Old World's gold supply. The Bambuk, Bure, and Akan gold fields supplied gold that flowed northward to mint coins in the Islamic world and Europe. Gold was the most valuable commodity, driving the entire trade system.

### Salt
Extracted from mines at Taghaza (Mali) and Taoudenni, salt was essential for preserving food and maintaining health in the hot climate. Salt was traded at roughly equal weight to gold in many markets, making it one of the most valuable commodities.

### Other Goods
- **Ivory:** From elephant tusks, highly valued in North Africa and the Middle East
- **Kola nuts:** Stimulant from West African forests, traded northward
- **Textiles:** Manufactured goods from North Africa and the Middle East
- **Slaves:** Enslaved people were among the most tragic commodities traded across the Sahara

## Economic and Cultural Impact

The trans-Saharan trade had profound effects on West Africa:

- **Rise of Empires:** Control of trade routes enabled the rise of the Ghana, Mali, and Songhai empires.
- **Urbanization:** Cities like Timbuktu, Gao, and Djenne grew into major commercial and intellectual centers.
- **Islamization:** Muslim traders brought Islam to West Africa, which rulers adopted for diplomatic and economic advantages.
- **Cultural Exchange:** Arabic language, Islamic scholarship, and architectural styles spread southward.
- **Wealth Accumulation:** West African states accumulated enormous wealth, with Mansa Musa's pilgrimage to Mecca (1324) famously demonstrating Mali's gold reserves.

## Decline

Trans-Saharan trade began its decline in the 16th century with the advent of European maritime trade along the West African coast. Portuguese traders bypassed the Sahara, establishing direct sea routes to Europe. However, the trade network persisted in modified forms well into the 19th century.

## Significance

Trans-Saharan trade represents one of Africa's most important economic and cultural systems. It connected sub-Saharan Africa to the wider world, facilitated the rise of great African empires, and contributed to the economic development of both Africa and the Mediterranean world. The trade routes also enabled the spread of Islam and Islamic scholarship, leaving a lasting cultural and religious legacy across West Africa.
