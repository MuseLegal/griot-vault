## Summary
Great Zimbabwe (c. 1100–1450 AD) is the largest ancient structure in sub-Saharan Africa, built by the ancestors of the Shona people. Located in present-day Zimbabwe, 30 km from Masvingo, the site covers approximately 800 hectares and consists of three architectural complexes: the Hill Complex, the Great Enclosure, and the Valley Ruins. At its peak, the city housed 10,000–20,000 people.

## Context
Built between the 11th and 15th centuries using dry-stone masonry (stones layered without mortar), Great Zimbabwe was the heart of a thriving trading empire connecting inland Africa to Indian Ocean trade. The Great Enclosure features the iconic cone (100m × 80m) and walls reaching 11 meters high — the largest ancient structure south of the Sahara. The site includes soapstone "Zimbabwe Birds," which became the national emblem of modern Zimbabwe.

## Significance
Great Zimbabwe disproves colonial-era claims that African stone architecture was impossible without foreign builders. The city was a major urban center in the medieval world, controlling gold, ivory, and Indian Ocean trade. It remains a sacred site for local communities (Charumbira, Mugabe, Murinye, Nemanwa clans) who continue spiritual practices there. UNESCO designated it a World Heritage Site in 1986.
