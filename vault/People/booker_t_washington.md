## Summary

Booker T. Washington (1856–1915) was an American educator, author, and leader in the African American community. Born into slavery, he rose to become the most influential Black leader in the United States from the 1890s until his death. He founded the Tuskegee Normal and Industrial Art School (now Tuskegee University) in 1881 and advocated for vocational education and economic self-reliance as the path to racial progress.

## Early Life

Born into slavery on April 2, 1856, on the plantation of James Burroughs in Hale County, Alabama. His mother was Jane, an enslaved woman. After the Emancipation Proclamation (1863), Washington walked more than 400 miles to the Hampton Institute in Virginia to attend school, working menial jobs to support himself.

## Tuskegee Institute (1881–1915)

In 1881, Washington was appointed to lead the Tuskegee Normal and Industrial Art School in Alabama, a newly founded institution for training Black teachers. Under his leadership, Tuskegee grew into one of the most important Black educational institutions in the South:

- He recruited students from surrounding communities, often living in crude shacks on the campus.
- Students physically built many of Tuskegee's buildings, including the famous Tuskegee Chapel.
- The school emphasized practical skills: agriculture, carpentry, masonry, and domestic arts alongside basic academics.
- Tuskegee attracted philanthropic support from Northern industrialists, including Andrew Carnegie and Julius Rosenwald.

## The Atlanta Compromise (1895)

Washington's famous Atlanta Exposition Address on September 18, 1895, became known as the "Atlanta Compromise." In it, he urged Black Americans to focus on economic advancement through vocational training rather than immediate demands for political and social equality. He proposed that Southern whites would gradually extend certain civil rights in exchange for Black acceptance of segregation and economic subordination.

While widely praised by white Americans and Northern philanthropists, the speech was criticized by some Black intellectuals, including W.E.B. Du Bois, who argued it conceded too much to white supremacy.

## Influence and Controversy

Washington wielded enormous political influence, advising multiple U.S. presidents (including Theodore Roosevelt and William Howard Taft) on Black appointments and policies. He was the first Black man invited to the White House (1901).

However, his approach was controversial:
- **W.E.B. Du Bois** criticized Washington's accommodationist stance in "The Souls of Black Folk" (1903).
- The Niagara Movement (1905) and later the NAACP (1909) emerged in direct opposition to Washington's philosophy.
- Washington secretly funded legal challenges to segregation and voting restrictions.

## Literary Legacy

Washington's autobiography, *Up from Slavery* (1901), is one of the most important works of African American literature. It recounts his journey from slavery to educational leadership and remains widely read today.

## Death and Legacy

Booker T. Washington died on November 14, 1915, in Tuskegee, Alabama, at age 59. His legacy is complex:
- He established Tuskegee University, one of the most important HBCUs in America.
- The Rosenwald School program (1912–1932) built over 5,000 schools for Southern Black children, funded by Julius Rosenwald.
- His philosophy of economic self-reliance influenced generations of Black leaders.
- His accommodationist approach was challenged by the civil rights movement that followed.

## Significance

Booker T. Washington represents the tension between accommodation and resistance in African American history. His emphasis on education and economic self-reliance produced tangible results — Tuskegee University, thousands of Rosenwald Schools — while his political concessions to white supremacy were later challenged by the civil rights movement. Understanding Washington is essential to understanding the full spectrum of African American strategies for racial progress.
