## Summary

Harriet Tubman (c. 1822–1913), born Araminta Ross, was an American abolitionist, social activist, and political advocate. Known as the "Moses of her people," she escaped from slavery and made thirteen missions on the Underground Railroad, rescuing approximately 70 people — including family and friends — over twelve years. During the Civil War, she served as a spy, scout, and nurse for the Union Army, becoming the first woman to lead an armed assault during the conflict.

## Early Life and Escape from Slavery

Born into slavery around 1822 in Dorchester County, Maryland, to parents Harriet ("Rit") Green and Benjamin Ross, both enslaved. She endured brutal physical abuse from childhood, including a head injury from a weighted divider thrown by an overseer that caused lifelong seizures and hypersomnia.

In 1849, Tubman escaped to Philadelphia via the Underground Railroad, a network of safe houses and abolitionist helpers. She changed her name to Harriet, possibly after her mother. Within a year, she returned to Maryland to rescue her family.

## The Underground Railroad (1850–1862)

Tubman made approximately 13 trips back to the South between 1850 and 1862, guiding roughly 70 enslaved people to freedom in the North and Canada. She never lost a "passenger" and developed ingenious methods to evade capture:

- Traveling in winter to reduce the likelihood of patrol
- Using churches and abolitionist homes as safe houses
- Carrying a pistol to prevent anyone from turning back
- Using songs and coded messages to communicate

Her most famous rescue was of her elderly parents in 1857.

## Civil War Service (1861–1865)

Tubman's wartime service was groundbreaking:
- **Scout and Spy:** Gathered intelligence for the Union Army in South Carolina's Sea Islands.
- **Combahee River Raid (June 1, 1863):** Led the first armed assault in U.S. history by a woman, guiding Union gunboats to raid Confederate plantations and freeing more than 700 enslaved people.
- **Nurse:** Treated wounded soldiers at hospitals in South Carolina and Florida.

## Post-War Activism

After the war, Tubman struggled financially despite being promised a pension. She lobbied Congress for decades to receive compensation for her wartime service. In 1899, she successfully petitioned Congress for a military pension based on her service as a nurse.

She established the Harriet Tubman Home for Aged Colored People in Auburn, New York, where she lived with her husband, Nelson Davis (a Civil War veteran she married in 1869).

## Legacy

Harriet Tubman's legacy includes:
- Being the first woman honored with a U.S. postage stamp (1948)
- Her image was proposed for the $20 bill (though this plan has been delayed)
- Harriet Tubman Underground Railroad National Historical Park (established 2008)
- Recognition as one of the most important figures in American history

## Significance

Harriet Tubman represents the courage and determination of enslaved people who fought for their own freedom. Her actions on the Underground Railroad directly challenged the institution of slavery, and her Civil War service broke gender and racial barriers in American military history. She remains an enduring symbol of resistance against oppression.
