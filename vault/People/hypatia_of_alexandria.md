## Summary
Hypatia of Alexandria (c. 355–415 CE) was a mathematician, astronomer, and philosopher in Roman Egypt. She taught Neoplatonism in Alexandria, authored commentaries on Apollonius of Perga's "Conics" and Diophantus of Alexandria's "Arithmetic," and produced astronomical tables (possibly revising her father Theon's work on the "Almagest"). She was murdered by a Christian mob in 415 CE, an event symbolizing the end of classical Alexandria.

## Context
Hypatia was the daughter of Theon of Alexandria, a noted mathematician and last major scholar at the Library of Alexandria. She became head of the Neoplatonist school in Alexandria, teaching philosophy, mathematics, and astronomy to a diverse student body. She designed astrolabes and hydrometers, and was known for her intellectual independence and political influence in Alexandria's turbulent religious politics.

## Significance
Hypatia represents one of the earliest known women in mathematics and philosophy. Her commentaries on "Conics" and "Arithmetic" were foundational texts. Her murder by a Christian mob led by Cyril's followers marked a turning point in the decline of classical learning in Alexandria. She remains a symbol of intellectual freedom and the dangers of religious intolerance.
