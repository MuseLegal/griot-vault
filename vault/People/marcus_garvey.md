## Summary

Marcus Mosiah Garvey Jr. (1887–1940) was a Jamaican-born Pan-Africanist, businessman, and political leader who founded the Universal Negro Improvement Association (UNIA) in 1914. Garvey advocated Black nationalism, racial pride, economic self-sufficiency, and the "Back to Africa" movement. At its peak, the UNIA claimed millions of members worldwide and organized the Black Star Line, a shipping company intended to facilitate trade and migration between African diaspora communities.

## Early Life in Jamaica

Born August 17, 1887, in St. Ann's Bay, Jamaica, to Marcus Garvey Sr. and Sarah Jane Thomas. His parents were of mixed African and European descent. Garvey received minimal formal education but was deeply influenced by his father's stories of African resistance to colonialism.

In 1908, Garvey traveled to London as a clerk for the National Colony of Sierra Leone, where he was exposed to anti-colonial ideas and the writings of W.E.B. Du Bois and other Black intellectuals.

## Universal Negro Improvement Association (1914–1930s)

Garvey founded the UNIA in Jamaica in 1914, moving its headquarters to Harlem, New York, in 1916. The organization grew to become the largest mass movement in African American history:

- **Membership:** At its peak in the 1920s, the UNIA claimed 6 million members worldwide across 40 countries.
- **Black Star Line (1919):** A shipping company intended to facilitate trade and transportation between African diaspora communities. Despite financial mismanagement, it symbolized Black economic self-reliance.
- **Negro World (1918–1933):** Garvey's newspaper, distributed internationally, promoted Black pride, Pan-Africanism, and economic independence.
- **Declaration of Rights of the Negro Peoples of the World (1920):** A document asserting Black self-determination and equality.

## Key Ideas and Activities

### Pan-Africanism
Garvey believed all people of African descent shared a common destiny and should work together for economic and political advancement. He famously declared: "Africa for the Africans, at home and abroad."

### Economic Nationalism
Garvey promoted Black-owned businesses, including the Negro Factories Corporation, which operated grocery stores, a restaurant, tailoring shop, and other enterprises in Harlem.

### Back to Africa Movement
Garvey advocated for the return of African descendants to Africa, particularly Liberia and Ethiopia, as a means of achieving true self-determination. He supported the establishment of a Black homeland in Africa.

### Cultural Pride
Garvey promoted African cultural heritage, organized parades, and encouraged Black people to take pride in their physical appearance and cultural traditions.

## Legal Troubles and Imprisonment

In 1923, Garvey was convicted of mail fraud for selling Black Star Line stock — a charge widely regarded by historians as politically motivated. He served time at the Atlanta Federal Penitentiary (1925–1927) before being deported to Jamaica by President Calvin Coolidge in 1927.

## Later Life and Legacy

Garvey returned to Jamaica in 1927, where he was elected to the House of Assembly. He continued to promote Pan-Africanism until his death on January 6, 1940, in London.

Garvey's influence extends far beyond his lifetime:
- **Rastafari Movement:** Garvey is considered a prophetic figure in Rastafarianism; his statement "Look to Africa, where a black king shall be crowned" was interpreted as referring to Haile Selassie I of Ethiopia.
- **Civil Rights Movement:** His emphasis on Black pride and self-reliance influenced Malcolm X, the Black Power movement, and the Black Arts Movement.
- **Pan-Africanism:** His ideas laid the groundwork for African independence movements in the 1950s and 1960s.
- **Cultural Impact:** His vision of African unity continues to inspire African diaspora communities worldwide.

## Significance

Marcus Garvey represents the most ambitious attempt at Pan-African economic and political organization in the 20th century. Despite his legal troubles and the failure of the Black Star Line, his vision of Black self-determination, economic independence, and cultural pride influenced generations of African American and African leaders. His legacy as a Pan-Africanist icon endures in movements from Rastafarianism to contemporary African diaspora activism.
