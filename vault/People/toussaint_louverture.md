## Summary
Toussaint Louverture (1743–1803) was a former enslaved person who became the most prominent leader of the Haitian Revolution (1791–1804). Born on a plantation in Saint-Domingue (present-day Haiti), he rose to become Governor-General for life of Saint-Domingue. His military and political leadership led to the abolition of slavery in the colony and laid the groundwork for Haiti's independence.

## Context
Louverture was born into slavery on a plantation in Bréda, Saint-Domingue. He received limited education, learning to read and write, and worked as a coachman and herd boy. During the Haitian Revolution (1791–1804), he joined the rebel forces in 1793, initially fighting for Spain before switching to the French Republic after France abolished slavery in 1794. He defeated Spanish, British, and French forces, establishing autonomous rule over Saint-Domingue.

## Significance
Toussaint Louverture's achievements set the grounds for Haiti's final victory over France. The Haitian Revolution continued under his lieutenant, Jean-Jacques Dessalines, who led the Haitian forces to victory over the French army at Vertières on 18 November 1803. Saint-Domingue declared independence from France on 1 January 1804, when formerly enslaved Africans established a new sovereign state named Haiti. Following independence from French colonial rule, Haiti was the first independent black republic in the Americas and the second independent nation in the Western Hemisphere, after the United States. In 1825, after twenty one years of Haiti's independence, France recognized the Haitian sovereignty, but only after forcing the new nation to agree to pay an indemnity of 150 million gold francs. President Abraham Lincoln officially recognized Haiti in 1862, during the American Civil War; the decision marked a significant reversal of decades of U.S. policy and was connected to the long-term legacy of Toussaint Louverture and the Haitian Revolution.
