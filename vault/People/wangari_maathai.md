## Summary

Wangari Muta Maathai (1940–2011) was a Kenyan environmentalist, political activist, and professor who founded the Green Belt Movement in 1977. She became the first African woman to receive the Nobel Peace Prize (2004) for her contribution to sustainable development, democracy, and peace. The Green Belt Movement has planted over 51 million trees across Kenya, employing thousands of women in reforestation efforts.

## Early Life and Education

Born April 1, 1940, in Ihithe, Nyeri District, Kenya (then British East Africa), to a family of smallholder farmers. She was one of nine children. Her father, Muta Baraza, was a member of the Kikuyu council of elders and participated in the Mau Mau uprising against British colonial rule.

Maathai's educational achievements were groundbreaking:
- **1960:** First Kenyan woman to study abroad (to the United States).
- **1964:** Bachelor's degree in Biology, Mount St. Scholastica College, Kansas.
- **1966:** Master's degree in Biology, University of Pittsburgh.
- **1971:** Ph.D. in Veterinary Anatomy, University of Nairobi — the first East African woman to earn a doctorate.
- **1968–1969:** Research fellowship at the University of Vienna.

## The Green Belt Movement (1977–present)

Maathai founded the Green Belt Movement in 1977 under the auspices of the National Council of Women of Kenya. The movement addresses environmental degradation, poverty, and women's rights through tree planting:

- **Tree planting:** Over 51 million trees planted across Kenya.
- **Women's employment:** Over 30,000 women trained in forestry, food processing, and bee-keeping.
- **Environmental education:** Community-based programs teaching sustainable land management.
- **Democratic advocacy:** The movement became a voice for democratic reform in Kenya.

## Political Activism

Maathai's environmental work intersected with political activism:
- She opposed the Kenyan government's plan to build a 60-story skyscraper (the Pan非洲 Centre) in Uhuru Park, Nairobi, arguing it would destroy a vital green space.
- She was beaten by police during a 2003 protest against the development.
- She ran for president of Kenya in 2002, finishing third in a field of twelve candidates.
- She served in the Kenyan Parliament (2003–2007) and as Associate Minister of Environment and Natural Resources.

## Nobel Peace Prize (2004)

The Nobel Committee recognized Maathai "for her contribution to sustainable development, democracy, and peace." The Peace Prize citation specifically acknowledged:

> "Her work demonstrates that protecting the environment is intrinsically linked to building peace, because environmental degradation often leads to conflict over resources."

Her Nobel lecture emphasized the connection between environmental destruction, poverty, and conflict.

## Legacy

Wangari Maathai's legacy includes:
- The Green Belt Movement, now operating in 30+ African countries.
- The Wangari Maathai Memorial Centre in Nyeri, Kenya.
- The Wangari Maathai Institute for Peace and Environmental Justice.
- A crater on Venus named "Wangari" by the International Astronomical Union.
- Her memoir *Unbowed* (2006) remains a powerful account of African women's resistance.

## Significance

Wangari Maathai represents the intersection of environmentalism, women's rights, and democratic activism. Her work demonstrates that environmental protection is inseparable from social justice, economic development, and peace. As the first African woman Nobel Peace Prize laureate, she broke barriers for African women in global leadership and continues to inspire environmental activists worldwide.
