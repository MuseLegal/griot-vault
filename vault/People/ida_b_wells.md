## Summary

Ida Bell Wells-Barnett (1862–1931) was an American investigative journalist, sociologist, educator, and early leader in the civil rights movement. She was one of the founders of the NAACP and the leading activist against lynching during the late 19th and early 20th centuries. Wells documented lynching through investigative journalism, publishing *Southern Horrors* (1892) and *A Red Record* (1895), which debunked the myth that lynching was a response to Black men raping white women.

## Early Life

Born into slavery on July 16, 1862, in Holly Springs, Mississippi, to James and Linda Wells, enslaved people who secured her freedom under the Emancipation Proclamation. When a yellow fever epidemic killed both her parents and her infant brother in 1878, 16-year-old Wells took responsibility for her younger siblings, working as a dressmaker and teacher to support the family.

## Journalism and Activism

Wells became a journalist and co-owner of *The Memphis Free Speech and Headlight* newspaper in 1889. Her reporting exposed racial injustice and segregation.

### The People's Grocery Incident (1892)
Three Black men — Thomas Moss, Calvin McDowell, and Henry Stewart — were lynched by a white mob in Memphis. They had been operating a successful grocery store that competed with a white-owned store. The lynching, which occurred on April 16, 1892, opened Wells's eyes to the true nature of lynching: it was about economic competition and racial terror, not sexual assault.

### Anti-Lynching Crusade
Wells published a series of articles exposing the truth about lynching:
- **Southern Horrors (1892):** Her first pamphlet, documenting the false justifications for lynching.
- **A Red Record (1895):** A detailed statistical account of lynchings, showing that most were motivated by economic competition, not sexual assault.
- **The Trail of Terror (1897):** A follow-up documenting continued lynchings.

When a white mob destroyed the office of her newspaper, Wells was forced to leave Memphis. She continued her activism in Chicago, where she moved with her husband, Ferdinand L. Barnett (a lawyer and activist she married in 1895).

## International Advocacy

Wells traveled to Europe multiple times (1893, 1900, 1911) to speak about lynching and racial injustice:
- **1893 London:** She spoke at the International Colonial Exhibition, challenging European attitudes toward Africa.
- **1900 Paris:** She attended the International Congress of Africa, presenting a paper on American lynching.
- **1911 Rome:** She spoke at the International Feminist Union Congress.

## Civil Rights Organizations

Wells was a founding member of several organizations:
- **National Association for the Advancement of Colored People (NAACP):** Co-founder in 1909, though she later withdrew her membership due to disagreements with white leadership.
- **National Association of Colored Women (NACW):** Active member and organizer.
- **Alpha Suffrage Club (1913):** Founded the first Black women's suffrage organization in Chicago.

## Political Candidacy

In 1930, at age 68, Wells ran for the Illinois State Senate as a Republican — one of the first Black women to run for political office in Illinois. She finished third in the primary but remained active in civil rights until her death.

## Legacy

Ida B. Wells's legacy includes:
- Pioneering investigative journalism that exposed racial terrorism.
- Founding the NAACP, the most important civil rights organization in American history.
- Establishing the Alpha Suffrage Club, empowering Black women politically.
- Her anti-lynching work laid the groundwork for the Civil Rights Act of 1964.
- The Ida B. Wells Society, a student organization at the University of North Carolina.
- A U.S. postage stamp honoring her (1995).

## Significance

Ida B. Wells represents the courage and intellectual rigor of African American women who fought against racial terrorism through journalism and activism. Her investigative work exposed the lies used to justify lynching and laid the intellectual foundation for the civil rights movement. Her work as a journalist, educator, and political organizer makes her one of the most important figures in American history.
